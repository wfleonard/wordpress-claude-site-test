Page builders promise that you can make any page you want. In practice you can make any page their designers anticipated, wrapped in layers of extra markup that slow everything down. When a client needs something genuinely different, the builder becomes the obstacle.

Start from the requirement, not the template

Our projects begin with a conversation about what a page has to accomplish. The answers are rarely generic:

  • A municipal bond platform needs deal pages with approval workflows and investor analytics.
  • A pipe band needs an events calendar, booking requests, a lessons page and a members only area.
  • A shop selling authenticated canvases needs product pages that explain certification as clearly as they show price.
  • A drilling contractor needs to show capability and service area to engineers in a hurry.

Each of those got pages designed for that exact job. Some are hand built in HTML, CSS and JavaScript; others, like MuniHub, run on WordPress. Either way, the structure comes from the requirement, not from a template, and the client can still update the words and images that change most.

On WordPress: editable, not fragile

We build with the WordPress block editor and our own block styles. The pricing cards on our pricing page, for example, are ordinary WordPress columns with a "Pricing card" style from our theme. Anyone on your team can change a price, add a feature or reorder plans without touching code, and nothing breaks when a plugin updates, because there is no plugin.

Accessible and fast by default

Custom does not mean careless. Every page we build checks color contrast against accessibility guidelines, works with a keyboard, reads sensibly to screen readers and stays light enough to load quickly on a phone.
